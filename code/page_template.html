    Some beaches give you the whole show: the Sun sliding down into an empty sea. Others hide it behind a headland, an island or the far side of a bay. On most of the east coast the sea sunset never comes, but the sunrise does. Which beaches in Britain and Ireland have a sea sunset, or a sea sunrise, and on which days of the year? I traced the Sun's path from {{n.coast|int}} named beaches to find out.

    01 · The mapSunset coasts and sunrise coasts

    Most beaches get one or the other, and a fifth get neither: {{neither_pc|pc}} of beaches face a bay, an estuary or a firth where land closes every view of the horizon at sunset and sunrise. {{set_only_pc|pc}} get a sea sunset but never a sea sunrise, and {{rise_only_pc|pc}} the reverse. Wales faces the sunset ({{nations.Wales.set_any_pc|pc}} of its beaches), the east coasts of England and Ireland the sunrise: Longsands at Tynemouth, Killiney in Dublin and {{all_year_rise_counties.Wexford|int}} beaches in Wexford and {{all_year_rise_counties.Wicklow|int}} in Wicklow see the Sun come up out of the sea on every day of the year.

    02 · The yearThe Sun swings along the horizon

    At midsummer the Sun sets far to the north-west and rises far to the north-east; at midwinter it sets in the south-west and rises in the south-east. So a beach that faces west may get its sea sunsets only in spring and autumn, with land at both ends of its view. In Cornwall, Sennen Cove looks out on open Atlantic all year round, and Fistral at Newquay gets {{cases.fistral_beach.sea_set_days|int}} sea sunsets but none in deep winter, when the Sun goes down behind the headland. On 21 December, {{set.dec21|int}} beaches have a sea sunset and {{rise.dec21|int}} a sea sunrise; on 21 June only {{set.jun21|int}} and {{rise.jun21|int}}. In winter the Sun rises and sets further south, and more beaches have an open view that way.

    03 · IslandsArran, Lundy and the Isle of Man

    Islands take a bite out of the year. From Troon on the Ayrshire coast the Isle of Arran hides the sunset on {{cases.troon.set_islands.Isle of Arran|int}} days, through spring and autumn. Lundy, small and about 30 km out in the Bristol Channel, takes {{cases.woolacombe_sands.set_islands.Lundy|int}} sunsets a year from Woolacombe and {{cases.croyde_beach.set_islands.Lundy|int}} from Croyde, around the equinoxes, when the Sun goes down due west. The Isle of Man hides {{cases.fleswick_bay.set_islands.Isle of Man|int}} sunsets from Fleswick Bay under St Bees Head, and {{cases.gilpin_harbour.rise_islands.Isle of Man|int}} sunrises from the beaches of County Down. The Isle of Wight and Mull share the record, each standing in the way for {{island_top_n|int}} beaches: the Isle of Wight for {{iow.set_beaches|int}} at sunset and {{iow.rise_beaches|int}} at sunrise, along the Solent and the Sussex coast.

    Irish Sea beaches on the English side, like Crosby ({{cases.crosby_beach.sea_set_days|int}} sea sunsets) and Half Moon Bay at Heysham ({{cases.half_moon_bay.sea_set_days|int}}), look west over open water for most of the year. At Crosby the Sun sets behind the Wirral and North Wales in the months around midwinter; at Half Moon Bay it is the other way round, and the summer Sun sets behind the Furness and Cumbrian coast to the north-west. The Isle of Man, over 100 km away, is below the horizon from there. Blackpool's own beach is not among the named beaches in the data.

    04 · How I did thisRays across the sea

    Beaches. Every beach in OpenStreetMap with a name in the United Kingdom and Ireland, {{n.osm_pieces|int}} pieces merged into {{n.beaches|int}} beaches where pieces of the same name lie within a kilometre. {{n.inland|int}} are on lakes or rivers, more than 600 m from the sea, and are left out. For each beach I put an observer at the water's edge, with eyes 3 m above the sea, at whichever point along the beach has the widest view of the year's sunrise and sunset bearings, because you can walk along a beach.

    Rays. From that point I cast a ray every quarter of a degree across the bearings where the Sun rises and sets in the year, out to {{model.far_km|int}} km. For the first {{model.near_km|int}} km the sea is OpenStreetMap's coastline, from the OpenFreeMap tiles at a resolution of about 20 to 25 m; beyond, land is wherever the AWS terrain tiles are above sea level. A piece of land hides the Sun only if it shows above the sea horizon, allowing for the Earth's curve and the usual bending of light near the ground: low land 50 km away is out of sight ({{model.hidden_50km_m|int}} m of it is hidden below the horizon), but the mountains of Arran are not. Then for every day of 2026 I worked out the bearing of sunset and sunrise and asked whether its ray reaches open horizon. The islands are named from OpenStreetMap and Natural Earth.

    What this cannot say. The weather, mostly. A sea sunset here means a clear line of sight to the sea horizon at the right bearing, not a cloudless evening. The coastline is the line of mean high water, so at low tide in places like Morecambe Bay the "sea" in front of you is sand. Refraction near the horizon varies from night to night, which moves the Sun by up to about half a degree, enough to tip a marginal day either way. Cliffs behind a beach do not matter for a sunset in front of it, but the headlands at its ends do, and from a long beach the answer depends on where you stand; I took the best spot. Only named beaches are mapped.
